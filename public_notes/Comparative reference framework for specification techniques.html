Intro

No single specification technique is universally optimal across all project typologies.

A comparative reference framework is therefore required to support the objective evaluation of specification techniques, helping to decide which one to use in a particular project.

This proposed comparative reference framework for specification techniques considers seven fundamental aspects, with the goal of being an initial reference point when discussing and evaluating different specification techniques.

In the field, other evaluation and quality frameworks exist, such as the SEQUAL Framework.

The next section describes the seven aspects of this framework: Aspect 1: Epistemic Clarity, Ambiguity Reduction, and Verifiability Aspect 2: Syntactic Rigor versus Stakeholder Comprehensibility Aspect 3: Expressiveness, Concurrency, and Systemic Modeling Aspect 4: Regulatory Compliance and Safety-Critical Mandates Aspect 5: Traceability, ALM Integration, and OSLC Interoperability Aspect 6: Economic Viability, Adoption Friction, and Agile Resilience Aspect 7: Synergies with Artificial Intelligence and Automated Evaluation

7 Aspects to compare specification techniques

Aspect 1: Epistemic Clarity, Ambiguity Reduction, and Verifiability

The foundational aspect for evaluating a specification technique is its capacity to enforce epistemic clarity: knowing precisely what is actually known, how it is known and where the knowledge ends. It requires a clear distinction between facts and inference, structured justification tracking and calibrated confidence levels.

In other words, it refers to how effectively the technique captures a requirement such that it yields one, and only one, valid interpretation.

This aspect assesses how effectively a technique prevents linguistic ambiguity, enforces completeness, and facilitates objective verification.

Aspect 2: Syntactic Rigor versus Stakeholder Comprehensibility

This second comparative aspect addresses the human communicative realities of requirements elicitation, commonly referred to as the “vocabulary gap”.

The aspect evaluates the cognitive load required to read, interpret, understand, validate, and negotiate the specification, weighing the specification technique’s level of syntactic rigor against its accessibility to its intended stakeholders.

Generally, there is an inverse relationship between mathematical rigor and human comprehensibility.

A specification technique is functionally useless if the target audience (e.g. domain experts, business sponsors, legal departments, regulatory authorities) who must validate the requirements cannot comprehend them.

Aspect 3: Expressiveness, Concurrency, and Systemic Modeling

This aspect acknowledges that not all software systems require the same type of information to be specified.

For instance, a web-based e-commerce platform relies primarily on user journey flows, whereas a real-time control system is based on microsecond timing constraints, hardware interrupts, and continuous state monitoring.

This third aspect then measures a specification technique’s semantic richness: its capacity to accurately express requirements including, for instance, complex conditional logic, concurrent operations scenarios, timing constraints, non-functional requirements and system architectures.

Aspect 4: Regulatory Compliance and Safety-Critical Mandates

In numerous industries, the selection of a requirements specification technique is not a free choice but is heavily constrained by external regulations.

This aspect measures a technique’s ability to satisfy external regulatory audit, compliance, and safety mapping requirements.

Aspect 5: Traceability, ALM Integration, and OSLC Interoperability

Software requirements, in general, exist in the context of a software development lifecycle. They undergo continuous evolution through incremental or recursive refinement and validation steps.

This aspect evaluates how effectively a specification technique scales across different sizes and shapes of project teams, its integration into Application Lifecycle Management tools, and its support for bidirectional traceability through the different types of requirements.

Aspect 6: Economic Viability, Adoption Friction, and Agile Resilience

The theoretical and mathematical superiority of a specification technique is ultimately irrelevant if its implementation is economically prohibitive or culturally incompatible with the engineering organization.

This aspect examines the Return on Investment (ROI) of a given specification technique, its costs, training requirements, and the friction associated with integrating the technique into an existing organizational culture.

It also assesses the technique’s resilience to rapid, volatile changes in scope.

Aspect 7: Synergies with Artificial Intelligence and Automated Evaluation

This aspect measures a specification technique’s compatibility with automated AI analysis, synthetic generation, and heuristic validation tools.

The intersection of GenAI and LLMs, and Requirements Engineering represents a critical and highly disruptive evaluation frontier.
